# Extraction checklist

Count these. Do not estimate them. The gap between what you believe about someone's writing and what they actually do is the whole reason this step exists.

Work through 25 to 40 pieces. Keep a running tally and collect verbatim examples as you go.

## Sentence and paragraph shape

- Mean sentence length, in words. Calculate it separately for internal email, external email, and long-form.
- Longest and shortest sentence you can find. The range matters as much as the average.
- Do they use fragments? How often, and in what register?
- Mean paragraph length, in sentences. Do they write one-line paragraphs, or blocks?
- Do they use line breaks between paragraphs, or run them together?

## Openings

- Greeting used, and how it varies by recipient. Collect every variant with a count. "Hi Sarah" 14, "Sarah," 6, "Hi all" 5, no greeting 3.
- Do they use the recipient's name at all?
- First sentence: do they open with the point, with a pleasantry, or with context?
- Count how many emails open with the ask. This one number tells you more about their register than any adjective.

## Closings

- Sign-off variants with counts. "Best" 20, "Thanks" 9, initials only 4, nothing 7.
- Do they sign with a name, initials, or nothing?
- Is there a closing line before the sign-off, or does the last substantive sentence end it?

## Vocabulary

- The 20 words and phrases that recur across pieces and are not common English. These are the fingerprint.
- Their word for common things: is it "call" or "catch-up" or "sync"? "Deck" or "presentation"? "Team" or "guys" or "all"?
- Jargon they use naturally, and jargon they conspicuously avoid.
- Any word that appears in almost every piece. Everyone has one.
- Regional spelling and conventions. Organise or organize. Date format. 24-hour clock or am/pm.

## Grammar and mechanics

- Contraction rate. Do they write "don't" or "do not"? Count both, and check whether it shifts with formality.
- First person: "I" or "we"? This is the most revealing single measure for an executive. Count the ratio, and check whether it changes when the news is bad.
- Active or passive, particularly when delivering something unwelcome.
- Exclamation marks per 1,000 words. Often zero, which is a hard rule you now have evidence for.
- Question marks: do they ask, or instruct?
- Em dashes, semicolons, ellipses, brackets. Note the ones they never use.
- Capitalisation habits in subject lines and headings.
- Emoji: never, rarely, or a specific small set?

## Structure

- Do they use bullets, or write in prose?
- Numbered lists: yes or no?
- Bold or underline for emphasis?
- Where does the ask sit: first line, last line, or buried?
- Typical email length in words, by type.
- Do they reply inline, top-post, or rewrite?

## Behaviour patterns

These are the ones that make a draft convincing.

- **How they say no.** Collect every decline in the corpus verbatim. Do they give a reason? Offer an alternative? Apologise? This is the highest value thing on this list, because assistants draft declines constantly and getting them wrong is expensive.
- **How they chase.** Do they reference the earlier message, restate the deadline, escalate?
- **How they praise.** Public or private, specific or general, and how much.
- **How they disagree.** Directly, by asking a question, or by going quiet and picking up the phone?
- **How they deliver bad news.** Lead with it, or build to it?
- **How they ask for something.** Imperative, question, or hedged?
- **Response length by importance.** Some people write longer when it matters, others get shorter.
- **What they answer in one line.** Look for the pattern in what earns a full reply and what earns "Yes, go ahead."

## Register shifts

Build a small table. For each pair, note what changes:

- To a direct report, against to a board member
- Internal, against external
- Good news, against bad
- Considered, against typed on a phone between meetings
- To someone they know well, against a first contact

The differences are the profile. A single voice description will produce drafts that are wrong half the time.

## Absence

List what never appears anywhere in the corpus. Absence across 30 samples is stronger evidence than anything you will be told.

Look specifically for: exclamation marks, emoji, apologies, hedging words, superlatives, jargon of a particular kind, questions, first-person plural, humour, greetings, sign-offs.

## Samples to keep

Pull 15 to 20 short verbatim examples and keep them with the profile, stripped of names, figures and confidential detail. Include:

- 3 openings, across different registers
- 2 declines
- 2 chases
- 2 pieces of bad news
- 2 short replies
- 1 long considered piece
- 1 piece of praise
- Anything that made you think "that is so him"

The samples do more work than the rules. When a draft comes out wrong, reading three real ones fixes it faster than re-reading the analysis.
